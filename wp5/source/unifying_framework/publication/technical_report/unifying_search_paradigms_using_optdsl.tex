\begin{abstract}
In practical combinatorial optimization, solver-paradigm decisions are frequently made at an early project stage.
Such early commitments constrain subsequent modeling choices and can render later paradigm changes technically and organizationally costly.
This report addresses that limitation through a workflow that enables paradigm switching over the full project lifecycle.
The workflow is based on \texttt{OptDSL} as a shared problem representation, leveraging its existing MiniZinc translation and extending the same representation with a Large Neighborhood Search (LNS) backend.
Beyond switching between the exact and the heuristic paradigm, the same model-centric representation provides the substrate for automated operator development: the construction, destroy, and repair operators that drive the LNS search can themselves be generated from the \texttt{OptDSL} model rather than being written by hand.
We detail the LNS design, including operator profiles, initialization strategies, and restart mechanisms, and evaluate the approach on benchmark instances from multiple problem classes.
Under fixed iteration and time budgets, we compare LNS-based heuristic search with a MiniZinc-based exact baseline on identical \texttt{OptDSL} instances, and we further demonstrate practical applicability on a real-world use case within an extensible, model-driven optimization workflow.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Combinatorial optimization practitioners routinely face a tension between solution quality, computation time, and implementation effort.
In many real projects, model requirements evolve over time, while the decision for a solver paradigm is made early and is difficult to revise later.
This creates a methodological lock-in that reduces both technical flexibility and practical efficiency.

Exact methods provide strong guarantees, most notably provable optimality and bounds on solution quality.
These properties are essential when results must be validated formally.
However, exact methods also exhibit well-known drawbacks.
Their computational cost can grow prohibitively on large or highly constrained instances, so that no acceptable solution may be available within practical time limits.
Their performance is sensitive to formulation quality, solver configuration, and available hardware, which makes results difficult to predict in advance.
Moreover, tight integration with a specific solver technology increases the engineering cost of model changes and rapid iteration.

Heuristic and metaheuristic methods occupy the opposite end of this trade-off.
They typically deliver good solutions quickly, scale to large instances, and can exploit domain structure through tailored search strategies.
This makes them attractive under strict runtime limits or in settings with frequent re-optimization.
Their drawbacks mirror their strengths: they provide no global optimality guarantees and usually no quality bounds, so the gap to the optimum remains unknown.
Their performance depends strongly on design choices such as operators, initialization, restart policies, and parameter settings, and a configuration that works well for one problem class may transfer poorly to another.
Consequently, substantial development and tuning effort is often required before a heuristic becomes reliable in practice.

Neither paradigm dominates the other; their strengths and weaknesses are largely complementary.
Which paradigm is preferable typically changes over the course of a project, for example when instance sizes grow, when time budgets shrink, or when formal quality guarantees become necessary.
This observation motivates a unifying approach in which the problem is modeled once and the solution paradigm remains exchangeable.

\texttt{OptDSL}, a Python-inspired domain-specific language for describing optimization problems, provides such a unifying representation.
A single \texttt{OptDSL} model can be dispatched to conceptually different backends:
an exact backend, currently realized through a translation to the constraint modeling language MiniZinc and its portfolio of solvers, and
a heuristic backend, currently realized through a Large Neighborhood Search (LNS) framework that operates directly on \texttt{OptDSL} decision variables.
Because both backends consume identical problem descriptions, users can defer the paradigm decision, switch paradigms as project requirements evolve, and compare both paradigms under a common protocol without re-modeling: the exact side translates the model into the MiniZinc representation, and the heuristic side imports it and operates directly on the \texttt{OptDSL} decision variables inside the LNS framework.
Beyond this dispatch view, the architecture admits a forward-looking perspective, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:optdsl-validation}: a solution that the LNS backend writes back in \texttt{OptDSL} format can be re-injected into the exact pipeline, where the MiniZinc representation and its solvers are used to validate and improve the solution and to derive quality bounds. This closes the loop between heuristic and formal optimization within one consistent, model-centric workflow.
In the figure, the dashed line together with the gray-shaded area marks this feedback loop (writing a solution back in \texttt{OptDSL} format and passing it to the exact backend for validation and improvement); this step is performed outside the scope of the present technical report, which provides the component optimizers but not yet an automated orchestration of the loop.
The blue box, by contrast, denotes the LNS-based heuristic part; it is the component that this report evaluates, and its full integration into the feedback loop is intended to be automated in subsequent work.

This report focuses on the design and evaluation of the LNS backend within this unified architecture.

We describe the implemented operator profiles, initialization strategies, and restart mechanisms, and evaluate the resulting search behavior on benchmark instances from multiple problem classes against the MiniZinc-based exact backend on identical \texttt{OptDSL} instances.
The objective is twofold: to study how far adaptive LNS design can close the quality gap under fixed compute budgets, and to demonstrate the practical value of a model-centric workflow in which the solver strategy can be revised throughout the project lifecycle.

The shared representation also unlocks a more ambitious direction: fully automated operator development.
Because the LNS backend consumes the \texttt{OptDSL} model directly, the ingredients of the search---the constructive initializer, and the destroy and repair operators---do not have to be designed by hand.
Instead, they can be generated from the model itself, validated, and iteratively improved within the unifying workflow.
This extends the idea of \emph{autoformulation} \citep{blaimschein2026autoformulation}---the LLM-based generation of an \texttt{OptDSL} formulation from a problem description in prose---from the model to the search: the same model-centric setting is used to also produce the search machinery that operates on the model.
In this report, the operator profiles are still co-developed by a human with LLM assistance to isolate and study the behavior of the LNS backend itself; the fully automated development of these operators is the subject of ongoing work and a central next step.

\section{Related Work}
Research on combinatorial optimization has increasingly moved from a strict exact-versus-heuristic dichotomy toward hybrid algorithms that combine the strengths of both paradigms.
In exact optimization, rich modeling languages and solver portfolios have improved accessibility and reproducibility, with MiniZinc becoming a standard interface for expressing models independently of the backend solver \citep{nethercote2007minizinc,stuckey2014minizinc}.
In parallel, neighborhood-based metaheuristics such as Large Neighborhood Search (LNS) with its destroy-and-repair paradigm are well suited to large-scale, real-world combinatorial optimization problems with complex constraints, where parts of a solution are iteratively removed and rebuilt in a feasibility-preserving way \citep{shaw1998lns,ropke2006alns,pisinger2010lns,pisinger2019lns}.

An important algorithmic line that narrows the quality-runtime gap is the use of exact neighborhoods or exact reasoning inside heuristic search loops.
In mixed-integer optimization, local branching and relaxation-induced neighborhoods are representative bridge mechanisms: they constrain search to a promising region that can still be solved (approximately or exactly) by mathematical programming machinery \citep{fischetti2003localbranching,danna2005rin}.
The same principle appears in CP-LNS workflows, where exact propagation and constraint reasoning guide neighborhood repair, while the outer search remains heuristic \citep{shaw1998lns,pisinger2010lns,pisinger2019lns}.
Conceptually, these methods trade global proof guarantees for faster high-quality improvement, but recover part of exact-method robustness through bounded or solver-driven subproblems.

A prerequisite for such cross-paradigm studies is a shared problem representation that both exact and heuristic solvers can consume.
\texttt{OptDSL} addresses this need: it is a Python-inspired domain-specific language for formulating optimization problems, and a translation from \texttt{OptDSL} to MiniZinc has been described and published \citep{delrio2026optdsl}, enabling \texttt{OptDSL} instances to be solved by any MiniZinc-compatible backend solver.
The present report builds directly on that work.
Rather than proposing a new modeling language or a new translation pipeline, it uses the existing \texttt{OptDSL} representation and MiniZinc translation as a foundation and contributes an experimental study that runs LNS-based heuristic search and MiniZinc-based exact solving on the same \texttt{OptDSL} instances.
This enables an apples-to-apples comparison of how much algorithmic gap can be closed through shared instance engineering and adaptive neighborhood design across multiple problem classes, within the bridge-algorithm tradition described above.

\section{Metaheuristic Search Framework}
\subsection{OptDSL Representation}
All benchmark instances are provided as standalone \texttt{OptDSL} models
in which data, decision variables, constraints, and the objective form a
single self-contained file.
Syntactically, an \texttt{OptDSL} model is a Python-inspired DSL
\citep{delrio2026optdsl}: scalar and array data are declared as typed
constants (e.g.\ \texttt{DSList(N, DSInt())} for integer arrays), decision
variables are declared with an explicit finite domain, feasibility
constraints are written as \texttt{assert} statements, and the objective
is declared as a final
\texttt{minimize(objective)} statement.
Because both backends consume exactly this file---the exact backend through
the translation to MiniZinc \citep{delrio2026optdsl}, the LNS backend
directly on the declared decision variables---no re-modeling is required
when switching between them.
We now summarize the model structure for the four benchmark classes; the
first three rely on linear arithmetic over bounded integer domains, whereas
woodcutting is expressed as a pipeline of record-transforming machines.

\textbf{Two-dimensional bin packing (2DBP).}
The model operates on three finite-domain decision-variable arrays
(\texttt{assignments}, \texttt{x\_positions}, \texttt{y\_positions}):
every item $i$ is assigned to a box $b$ and placed at a lower-left corner
$(x_i,y_i)$.
Feasibility is enforced by two constraint blocks: \texttt{all\_assigned}
ensures that each item receives exactly one box, and \texttt{no\_overlap}
first requires that each item fits within the dimensions of its selected
box and then requires, for any two items in the same box, that they do not
overlap in either the horizontal or the vertical direction.
The objective counts the number of distinct boxes in use and is minimized.

\textbf{Capacitated vehicle routing (CVRP).}
Two semantically equivalent encodings are used, which illustrates that
\texttt{OptDSL} permits representation choices tailored to the backend.
The \emph{arc-based} encoding declares a flat binary array
\texttt{arcs[v,i,j]} with index $v\cdot n^2 + i\cdot n + j$, where $v$
indexes the vehicle, $i$ the origin node, $j$ the destination node, and
$n$ the number of nodes including the depot; entries are one exactly when
vehicle $v$ travels from $i$ to $j$. Together with the arc array, the model
declares a binary \texttt{vehicle\_used} indicator and a variable $u$,
which is used for a Miller--Tucker--Zemlin formulation.
Constraints assert that every customer has in-degree and out-degree one
(\texttt{all\_customers\_visited}), that every used vehicle starts and ends
at the depot (\texttt{depot\_start\_end}), that flow is conserved at every
customer (\texttt{flow\_conservation}), that route demands respect the
vehicle capacity (\texttt{capacity\_constraints}), and that subtours
are excluded (\texttt{subtour\_elimination}); the objective sums the
traveled distance.
This encoding is used for the benchmark families distributed through the
CVRPLIB \citep{uchoa2017cvrplib} and is compatible with the MiniZinc
translation.
For the LNS backend on large instances we additionally use a
\emph{giant-tour} encoding with a permutation \texttt{tour} over customers
and a binary \texttt{cut\_after} array that marks route breaks at the
depot; constraints here are occurrence, route count, and a cumulative
capacity bound, and the objective inserts the depot detour at each cut.
This second encoding is intentionally LNS-oriented (it operates on the
permutation space of the route) and is not part of the MiniZinc baseline.

\textbf{Job shop scheduling (JSSP).}
The model declares finite-domain completion-time variables
\texttt{completion[j][op]} for every operation, bounded by
\texttt{MAX\_TIME}.
Two constraint blocks encode feasibility:
\texttt{precedence\_constraint} imposes the job-internal operation order
using the per-job \texttt{MACHINE\_SEQUENCE} and \texttt{PROCESSING\_TIMES},
and \texttt{no\_overlap\_constraint} asserts for every pair of operations
sharing a machine that one finishes before the other starts.
The objective is the makespan, i.e.\ the maximum completion time over all
jobs.
The exact backend solves the completion-time formulation directly.
The LNS repair operator instead works on the relative order of operations
per machine and derives an active schedule from that order; because an
optimal schedule is always recoverable from some machine order
\citep{applegate1991}, searching this order space does not sacrifice any
optimum.

\textbf{Woodcutting (industrial use case).}
The three classes above are flat constraint sets over decision variables.
The woodcutting problem, taken from an industrial sawing line, shows that
\texttt{OptDSL} also represents substantially more complex models.
Instead of a single constraint block, the model is a \emph{pipeline} of
machines that exchange whole record lists at module level: an optional
board-reordering machine, cutting, filtering, piece reordering, and a
checking machine.
The decision variables are the cut positions of every board
(\texttt{cuts\_list\_list}), a keep/discard bit per piece
(\texttt{keep\_decisions}), the piece-reordering bits
(\texttt{swapping\_decisions}), and, in the board-reordering variant, the
board-reordering bits (\texttt{swapping\_decisions\_boards}).
Piece reordering models a single-piece buffer, and board
reordering applies adjacent swaps from left to right.
The checking machine then imposes the beam constraints on the stream of kept
pieces: layer lengths, seam positions relative to the usable part of the beam
and to static forbidden zones, minimum seam distances between consecutive
layers, and an exact number of completed layers.
The objective is the total length of good material that is discarded.
This class is therefore not a toy formulation: it combines record-valued
data, list-transforming predicates, and side conditions that depend on the
order of the produced pieces, all expressed in the same \texttt{OptDSL}
syntax that the exact and the heuristic backend consume.
It is evaluated separately in Section~\ref{sec:woodcutting}.

\subsection{LNS Configuration}
The LNS backend instantiates the classical destroy-and-repair scheme of \citet{shaw1998lns} and combines it with the adaptive operator-selection and weight-update mechanism of ALNS \citep{ropke2006alns,pisinger2010lns,pisinger2019lns}.
It was not the goal to design a general LNS algorithm that works for every problem formulation in \texttt{OptDSL}; instead, the backend reads the \texttt{OptDSL} model and relies on operators that are customized and specialized for the specific problem at hand.
Operator profiles are realized per problem class; in particular, the job-shop repair operator constructs active schedules in the spirit of \citet{applegate1991}.
For each class, a complete profile consists of three complementary operators: a \emph{construction} operator that produces a first feasible assignment from the \texttt{OptDSL} model (an initializer), a \emph{destroy} operator that removes part of a current solution, and a \emph{repair} operator that restores a feasible complete assignment.
The profiles used in the computational study were co-developed with the assistance of a large language model.

The representation-based workflow, however, does not require manually developed operators: because the LNS backend reads the \texttt{OptDSL} model directly, the construction, destroy, and repair operators can be generated automatically from the model, validated against the shared semantics, and iteratively improved.
We call this \emph{autodevelopment} of the search machinery: a large language model reads the \texttt{OptDSL} model---its data, decision variables, constraints, and objective---and writes the construction, destroy, and repair operators as plain, self-contained Python that registers itself with the LNS engine.
Because the generated code uses the same registration interface as a hand-written profile, an automatically produced profile plugs into the exact same pipeline and is evaluated by the same engine.
This process is the subject of our current and future work; a first prototype generates candidate operators, checks them with a static and subprocess-based quality gate that executes the operator on the \texttt{OptDSL} model, and retains them only if they survive a holdout evaluation on seeds that were not used during generation.
Automated operator development is not the focus of this technical report, which instead concentrates on the human-supervised LNS backend and its evaluation.
In the present study this automated stage is left to subsequent work so that the human-supervised operators provide a controlled baseline.

\textbf{Search loop.}
Figure~\ref{fig:lns-design} shows how the engine combines the general loop with
the problem-specific profile.
The construction operator builds a first feasible assignment, which is
evaluated against the \texttt{OptDSL} model and becomes the current and the
incumbent solution.
Construction is profile-specific: depending on the class it either samples a
random feasible assignment or runs a randomized constructive heuristic several
times and keeps the best attempt.
Each iteration then selects a destroy/repair pair, removes part of the current
assignment, repairs it into a feasible candidate, and evaluates the candidate
against the model's own constraints and objective.
An acceptance criterion decides whether the candidate replaces the current
solution: either a strict improving rule, or a simulated-annealing rule that
also accepts worsening moves with a Metropolis probability and cools down over
time.
Independently of acceptance, the candidate replaces the incumbent whenever it
improves on it.
Operator selection is either uniform over the configured pairs or adaptive in
the ALNS sense: each pair carries a weight that is updated from a decayed
score, rewarding new best solutions, improvements over the current solution,
and accepted worsening moves, and the destroy intensity is scaled up when the
search stalls.
Stagnation can additionally trigger a restart, which re-runs the construction
operator and continues from the new assignment while keeping the incumbent.
The loop stops after a fixed number of iterations, when a time limit is
reached, or when no improvement has occurred for a given number of iterations.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The main objective of this research is to show that a problem formulated once in \texttt{OptDSL} can be used with two different backends, the exact MiniZinc-based backend and the LNS-based heuristic backend, and that both backends can work together on the same formulation.
The experimental setup is therefore organized around this goal: we first describe the benchmark families, then the baselines against which the two backends are compared.

The study currently uses small and medium-sized instances for the computational
comparison.
This restriction is deliberate: the aim is to demonstrate the feasibility of the unified workflow and the interaction of the two backends, not to establish a competitive benchmark result.
Larger instances are the subject of ongoing work, where the binding costs are the \texttt{OptDSL}$\to$MiniZinc translation on the exact side and the operator design on the heuristic side.

\subsection{Benchmark Families}
We evaluate on benchmark families from four problem classes, chosen for their complementary structure, their prevalence in the literature, and the availability of reference solutions.

\textbf{Capacitated Vehicle Routing (CVRP).}
We use the classical Golden set \citep{christofides1979,golden1998} and the \textsc{E-n} set of \citet{christofides1969}, which are also distributed through the CVRPLIB \citep{uchoa2017cvrplib}; see \citet{toth2014vrp} for a comprehensive treatment.

\textbf{Two-Dimensional Bin Packing (2DBP).}
We use the \textsc{n/t} instances of \citet{hopper2001genetic,hopper2001ejor}, the \textsc{Wang} instances \citep{wang1983}, and the instance classes introduced by \citet{berkey1987}; exact methods and bounds follow \citet{lodi2002twodimensional,martello1998exact}.
In addition, we use an internally generated uniform-bin test set (\texttt{2D-BPP\_CLASS}) that mirrors the \textsc{Wang}/\textsc{Berkey--Wang} class structure but is fully reproducible.

\textbf{Job Shop Scheduling (JSSP).}
We use the standard families \textsc{ta} \citep{taillard1993benchmarks}, \textsc{la} \citep{lawrence1984}, \textsc{abz} \citep{adams1988shifting}, \textsc{orb} \citep{applegate1991}, and \textsc{swv} \citep{storer1992}.
Reference makespans are taken from the published optima \citep{applegate1991,nowicki1996fast,taillard1993benchmarks}; see also the survey of \citet{jain1999jssp}.

\textbf{Woodcutting.}
The woodcutting class models a sawing line that cuts raw boards into pieces,
filters out unusable pieces, optionally reorders boards and pieces, and stacks
the remaining pieces into layers of beams of fixed length.
All instances are internally generated with a problem instance generator and
are stored together with the benchmark data.
Their properties are listed in Table~\ref{tab:woodcutting-instances}.
Because these models are written as a pipeline of record-transforming
machines rather than as a flat constraint set, they are reported separately in
Section~\ref{sec:woodcutting} rather than in
Table~\ref{tab:study-results}.

\subsection{Evaluation Baselines}
The quality of the LNS backend is assessed against the exact MiniZinc-based
backend on identical \texttt{OptDSL} instances.
On each instance, the exact backend either proves optimality or, when the
time limit is reached, yields a feasible incumbent together with an
optimality gap from its reported bound; on some instances it finds no feasible
solution at all, which we report as such.
This provides two complementary baselines: reference (optimal) values for
small instances, and a certified lower bound that limits the achievable gap of
the heuristic on the larger benchmark families.
Where published optima or best-known values exist, they serve as an
additional independent reference
\citep{applegate1991,nowicki1996fast,taillard1993benchmarks,uchoa2017cvrplib,martello1998exact}.

\section{Computational Study and Preliminary Results}
The computational study compares the LNS-based heuristic backend against the
MiniZinc-based exact backend on identical \texttt{OptDSL} instances under
fixed iteration and time budgets.
Only a selected subset of the available benchmark instances is used, restricted
to small and medium-sized instances.
This restriction is imposed by the current limitations of the
\texttt{OptDSL}$\to$MiniZinc translation, whose cost grows with the model size
and becomes the binding factor on larger instances.
Table~\ref{tab:study-results} summarizes the outcome for the benchmark
instances considered so far.
All rows follow the same protocol: one \texttt{OptDSL} file per instance, a
120\,s budget per backend, a single LNS seed, and the MiniZinc cp-sat backend
with the \texttt{OptDSL}$\to$MiniZinc translation included in the reported
MiniZinc time.
The \emph{Size} column is class-specific (cities/vehicles/capacity for CVRP,
jobs/machines for JSSP, bin dimensions/items for 2DBP).
For each instance we report the objective value found by each backend, the
bound reported by MiniZinc, the wall-clock time of each backend, and two
relative gaps: the MiniZinc gap
$(\text{MZ quality}-\text{MZ bound})/|\text{MZ bound}|$ and the LNS gap
$(\text{LNS}-\text{MZ bound})/|\text{MZ bound}|$.

\subsection{Results on the Literature Benchmarks}
Where known optima or best-known values are available, solution quality is benchmarked against them \citep{applegate1991,nowicki1996fast,taillard1993benchmarks,uchoa2017cvrplib,martello1998exact}.

\textbf{How to read the gap columns.}
The MiniZinc gap measures how far the exact backend's own incumbent is from
its reported bound, and it is undefined (\textit{---}) when MiniZinc found no
feasible solution.
The LNS gap is defined against the MiniZinc \emph{bound}, not against the
MiniZinc \emph{quality}, so it carries two different meanings depending on
whether MiniZinc closed the instance.
When quality and bound coincide, the bound is a proof of optimality and the
LNS gap is the true suboptimality of the LNS solution.
When they differ, the LNS gap is dominated by the weakness of the dual bound
and says nothing about how far the LNS solution is from the optimum.

\begin{sloppypar}
This distinction matters for most rows: MiniZinc closed only
\texttt{random\_euclidean\_cvrp\_8}, \texttt{jssp\_10x5},
\texttt{jssp\_2x2}, \texttt{jssp\_5x4},
\texttt{2d\_bpp\_inst\_cl\_06\_020\_10} and
\texttt{2d\_bpp\_inst\_cl\_05\_020\_08},
whereas on \texttt{E-n13-k4}, all \textsc{la} instances and \texttt{orb01}
the reported bound lies far below the incumbent, and on the two random
Euclidean CVRP instances and two 2DBP instances MiniZinc found no incumbent at
all.
The large gaps in the \textsc{la} block (56--86\,\%) must therefore not be
read as LNS being 56--86\,\% worse than the exact backend; they mainly
reflect that the MiniZinc bound stays far below its own incumbent
(e.g.\ 413 vs.\ 727 on \texttt{la01}).
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Capacitated vehicle routing (CVRP).}
On the small instances, \texttt{random\_euclidean\_cvrp\_8} is closed by both
backends at the same objective value (370), with MiniZinc proving optimality
in 2.2\,s and LNS needing 1.8\,s.
On \texttt{E-n13-k4} the picture reverses: LNS reaches 247, the known
optimum, in 3.6\,s, whereas MiniZinc returns 279 with a bound of 139 and does
not close the instance within its budget.
On the two medium instances with 15 and 20 cities, MiniZinc reports a bound of
274 and 300 but no incumbent within its budget, while LNS returns 555 and 554.
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{Job shop scheduling (JSSP).}
On the small instances, both backends return the same objective value on
\texttt{jssp\_2x2} (6) and \texttt{jssp\_5x4} (22), with MiniZinc proving
optimality in 0.8\,s and 1.2\,s respectively.
On \texttt{jssp\_10x5} MiniZinc proves the optimum of 37 and LNS returns 38,
i.e.\ 2.7\,\% above the proven optimum.
On the medium instances the exact backend closes none of the rows: on
\texttt{la01}--\texttt{la05} and \texttt{orb01} the reported bound lies far
below the incumbent, so the LNS gaps of 56--86\,\% must not be read as LNS
being that much worse than the exact backend.
The \textsc{la} rows are limited by the exact backend rather than by the
model: \texttt{chuffed} proves \texttt{la01}--\texttt{la05} optimal at 666,
655, 597, 590, and 593, i.e.\ exactly the published optima, whereas
\texttt{cp-sat} returns incumbents 2--18\,\% above them within the same
budget.

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Two-dimensional bin packing (2DBP).}
On the small instances, \texttt{2d\_bpp\_inst\_cl\_06\_020\_10} is closed by
both backends at objective $1$, whereas on
\texttt{2d\_bpp\_inst\_cl\_06\_020\_06} MiniZinc produced no feasible solution
within its budget and LNS found a one-box packing in 69\,s.
On the medium instances, \texttt{2d\_bpp\_inst\_cl\_05\_020\_08} is closed by
both backends at objective $5$, whereas on
\texttt{2d\_bpp\_inst\_cl\_04\_020\_08} MiniZinc found no feasible solution and
on \texttt{2d\_bpp\_inst\_cl\_10\_020\_09} it returned a solution (6 boxes)
but reported a bound of zero.
These failures are a limitation of the \emph{solver}, not of the model or the
translation: on \texttt{2d\_bpp\_inst\_cl\_06\_020\_06} the same generated
MiniZinc model is solved to proven optimality (objective $1$) by both
\texttt{gecode} and \texttt{chuffed}, whereas \texttt{cp-sat} returns
\textsc{unknown} with zero solutions after $30$\,s.
The disjunctive no-overlap encoding is therefore valid and solvable; it is
the \texttt{cp-sat} backend that fails to find a feasible packing on these
instances.
The corresponding $\infty$ entries are consequently not evidence of LNS
suboptimality; they mark a degenerate bound of zero.
For the 2DBP class we therefore recommend \texttt{gecode} or
\texttt{chuffed} over \texttt{cp-sat}.
The 2DBP rows are limited to $n=20$ because the \texttt{OptDSL}$\to$MiniZinc
translation becomes the binding cost beyond that size.
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{Runtime.}
LNS never exhausted its 120\,s budget (maximum 69\,s; every run terminated
by the internal stop criterion), whereas MiniZinc used its full budget on the
remaining twelve instances.
The comparison is thus not wall-clock matched.
On those twelve instances LNS is between about two and thirty-five times
faster (\texttt{la01}: 9\,s vs.\ 130\,s; \texttt{E-n13-k4}: 3.6\,s vs.\
124\,s), with the smallest speedups on two of the 2DBP instances
(2.3$\times$ and 2.6$\times$).
On the tiny instances the two runtimes are comparable and the ranking is not
stable (\texttt{jssp\_5x4}: 1.9\,s for LNS vs.\ 1.2\,s for MiniZinc), so the
remaining budget could be spent on additional iterations or restarts rather
than being read as a speed advantage.
Note that the reported MiniZinc time includes the \texttt{OptDSL}$\to$MiniZinc
translation, which dominates on the larger models: for \texttt{orb01}
(10$\times$10) the translation alone takes about 5\,min, so the 471\,s
reported for that row are mostly translation rather than search.

\textbf{Open issues.}
The study still uses a single seed and no independent feasibility check of
the LNS output, so the table should be read as a first indication of relative
behaviour rather than as a benchmark result.

\subsection{Woodcutting}
\label{sec:woodcutting}
The woodcutting class is not part of Table~\ref{tab:study-results} because its
models are structurally different from the other three: instead of a flat
constraint set over decision variables, they are written as a \emph{pipeline}
of ``machines'' that exchange whole record lists at module level, one machine
per processing stage.
We therefore document the class separately on its two smallest instances,
which are small enough to reason about by hand.

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Problem.}
A set of raw boards must be cut into pieces that are stacked into a given
number of beams, each consisting of a fixed number of layers of fixed length.
The boards carry defect intervals, so not every piece is usable, and the
stacking imposes geometric restrictions on where a layer may be closed.
The problem is to decide how to cut, select, and order the material so that
the beams are filled exactly while wasting as little good material as
possible.

The input fixes the geometry and the demand: the layer length
\texttt{BEAM\_LENGTH}, the number of layers per beam \texttt{BEAM\_DEPTH}, the
number of beams \texttt{N\_BEAMS}, the unusable start and end margins
\texttt{BEAM\_SKIP\_START} and \texttt{BEAM\_SKIP\_END}, the static forbidden
zones \texttt{FORBIDDEN\_INTERVALS}, and the minimum seam distance between
consecutive layers \texttt{GAP\_TO\_BOARD\_ABUT\_IN\_CONSECUTIVE\_LAYERS}.
It further fixes the board data (\texttt{N\_BOARDS}, \texttt{MAX\_BOARD\_LENGTH},
the defect intervals per board) and the discretisation
\texttt{MAX\_N\_CUTS\_PER\_BOARD}, from which the number of pieces
\texttt{N\_PIECES} follows.
All instances studied here use the same geometry:
\texttt{BEAM\_LENGTH} $=500$, \texttt{BEAM\_SKIP\_START} $=$
\texttt{BEAM\_SKIP\_END} $=10$, \texttt{GAP\_TO\_BOARD\_ABUT\_IN\_CONSECUTIVE\_LAYERS}
$=10$, and the four forbidden zones $[90,110]$, $[190,210]$, $[290,310]$ and
$[390,410]$; they differ in \texttt{N\_BEAMS}, \texttt{BEAM\_DEPTH}, the
boards, and the defects.

The problem decomposes into five stages, each of which contributes decisions
and constraints.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Board reordering} (optional) permutes the raw boards by adjacent
swaps applied from left to right, so that the cutting stage sees them in a
different order.
\item \emph{Cutting} splits every board at a bounded number of cut positions
into pieces. The first cut is fixed at the start margin and the last at the
board length minus the end margin, the cuts must be non-decreasing, and a
piece is good if it overlaps no defect interval.
\item \emph{Filtering} decides for every piece whether it is kept or
discarded. A bad piece can never be kept, and the length of every discarded
good piece is charged to the objective.
\item \emph{Piece reordering} models a single-piece buffer.
\item \emph{Checking} validates the stream of kept pieces against the beam
constraints: the running length of a layer never exceeds the layer length;
every seam that does not close a layer lies strictly inside the usable part of
the beam, outside the forbidden zones, and at least the required gap away from
the seams of the previous layer of the same beam; and at the end exactly
\texttt{N\_BEAMS} $\times$ \texttt{BEAM\_DEPTH} layers are completed with no
partial layer left.
\end{itemize}
The objective is the total length of good material that is discarded.
The input also declares \texttt{MAX\_PIECES\_PER\_BEAM},
\texttt{MIN\_DIST\_BETWEEN\_PIECES}, \texttt{MIN\_LENGTH\_OF\_BOARD\_IN\_LAYER}
and \texttt{MAX\_SHIFT\_CURVED\_CUT}, but the current model body does not use
them, so these rules are not enforced.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Instances.}
All instances are produced with a problem instance generator.
It takes the beam geometry (layer length, margins, forbidden zones, seam gap),
the number and length of the raw boards, and the defect rate as parameters,
and writes one JSON file per instance.
For a fixed random seed the generator is deterministic, so every instance is
reproducible.
Each generated file is converted to \texttt{OptDSL} by a converter script.
Table~\ref{tab:woodcutting-instances} lists the generated instances.
All of them share the beam geometry given above.
The instance \texttt{two\_boards\_two\_layers\_no\_faults} was designed as
the smallest non-trivial case: its usable material equals the demand exactly,
so the optimum wastes nothing (objective $0$), yet that optimum can only be
reached with a seam inside a layer, e.g.\ by cutting the $770$ board into
$500 + 250$ and the $270$ board into $250$, giving the layers $500$ and
$250 + 250$.
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{Experiments.}
The exact backend translates each \texttt{OptDSL} file to MiniZinc and solves
the resulting model with MiniZinc 2.10.1 using chuffed, gecode and cp-sat
(OR-Tools 9.15).
The time limit is $60$\,s for \texttt{two\_boards\_two\_layers\_no\_faults}
and $120$\,s for \texttt{one\_beam\_no\_faults}.
Reported MiniZinc times are wall-clock times of the MiniZinc process
(flattening and search); the \texttt{OptDSL}$\to$MiniZinc translation is not
included and takes $0.6$--$0.7$\,s and about $2$\,s, respectively.
The heuristic backend runs the woodcutting LNS profile
with $5000$ iterations on the small instance and with $20\,000$ iterations on
\texttt{one\_beam\_no\_faults}.
For the warm-start runs, LNS writes its incumbent as an
\texttt{OptDSL} file with a \texttt{@warm\_start} block
(option \texttt{--emit-feasible-dsl}); the block carries the cut positions and
the keep and reordering bits as solution hints and the incumbent's objective
as an upper bound.
We compare three variants: hints and bound, hints only, and bound only.

\textbf{Results on the small instance.}
Table~\ref{tab:woodcutting-small} shows that, with or without board
reordering, chuffed, cp-sat and LNS all reach the optimum $0$ in about one
second; chuffed and cp-sat also prove optimality, and LNS finds the optimum
as well.
gecode finds no solution within $60$\,s from a cold start, even though the
model has only $13$ (respectively $14$) decision variables.
A warm start helps gecode only through the objective bound: gecode warns that
it ignores the \texttt{warm\_start} search annotation, so the hints-only run
behaves like the cold start, whereas every run containing the bound proves the
optimum in under half a second.
This should not be overrated, because here the LNS bound already
equals the optimum, so it leaves gecode only the task of finding a waste-free
solution.
cp-sat solves the instance from a cold start, so a warm start changes little.
Board reordering does not change the outcome on this instance: no solver
chooses to swap the boards, because the optimum is reachable without a swap.

\section{Conclusion}
The study shows that a single problem formulation can be used across different
optimization paradigms.
The same \texttt{OptDSL} model is consumed by the exact MiniZinc backend and by
the LNS backend, and both produce solutions of the same semantics on identical
instances.
The two paradigms differ in what they require and in what they deliver.
The LNS backend needs an adaptation of the model: the search machinery, i.e.\
the construction, destroy, and repair operators, has to be provided per problem
class, which is the price for a fast search that reaches good solutions under a
fraction of the exact backend's budget.
The exact backend, in contrast, is generic: it translates the model and hands
it to a solver, which provides lower bounds and, where the search completes,
proofs of optimality.
The current limitation is on the exact side: the translation into MiniZinc
produces large models that are hard to solve, so on the larger instances the
solvers neither find good solutions nor prove optimality within the budget.
The most immediate next step is therefore to automate the construction of the
search machinery itself.
Building on the observation that the LNS backend consumes the model directly,
the construction, destroy, and repair operators can be generated from the model
by a large language model and validated within the same workflow, so that
switching paradigms eventually also automates the preparation of the heuristic
search.
This work is already in progress: a first prototype generates candidate
operators from the model and retains them only if they pass an automated
quality gate.
The implementation, the problem instance generator, the converters, and the
